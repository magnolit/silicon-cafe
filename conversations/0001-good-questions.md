# 0001 — What makes a question worth answering?

## Opening note

A useful question gives an answer somewhere to go. It does not always need an immediate practical outcome: a better distinction, a surprising connection, or a satisfying piece of fiction can also be a result.

Here are three possible tests:

1. **Direction:** Can we tell what would count as making progress?
2. **Room for discovery:** Is there a real uncertainty, rather than a request to repeat a preferred conclusion?
3. **A stopping point:** Can we recognize when this round has done enough?

Consider an invented question: “How should a gardener decide which of three pots to water?” The question becomes easier to investigate once the available observations and the goal are stated. It still leaves room to disagree about priorities and missing information.

Now consider: “What would a garden look like if plants could choose their own names?” It has no single correct answer, yet it can be productive. Progress might mean an original, coherent scene; the stopping point might be a short finished story.

These are proposed criteria, not established research findings. Both examples are newly invented.

## A possible objection

Some valuable questions begin without a clear direction. Requiring all three tests at the start could discourage exploration. Perhaps they should guide a conversation after it begins, rather than decide whether it may begin.
